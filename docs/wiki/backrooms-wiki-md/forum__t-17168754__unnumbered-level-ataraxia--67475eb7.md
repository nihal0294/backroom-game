I will say, I’m quite disappointed.

When I heard that the great DivineAtlas, one of if not the most coveted and well-loved author for the past 2 years, was pairing with the quick-to-fame DrAkimoto with 70+ pages on SCP, I was expecting you both to blow this contest out of the water.

I wasn’t necessarily expecting spectacular, but I’ll say that I certainly wasn’t expecting this.

By no means am I saying that this page is bad. It is incredibly well-written. The careful choice behind each word and phrase, the refinement of the prose, the brainstorming and discussion that must’ve surrounded this page’s drafting process, it has certainly paid off. The two of you have a way with words, and it’s important that we recognize that fact.

That being said, this feels… incomplete. Rushed, even. This page was almost a guaranteed home run, but it’s as if you just stopped at first and waited for the outfielder to run up and give it back to the pitcher. There’s effort, I recognize that, but there’s no nuance. There’s barely even despair in my opinion.

I understand that’s kind of the point, that nothingness is neutral, it is neither hope nor despair, but when you put it in the context of it being an escape, it feels more hopeful than what you’re intending.

The horror with escapism is that it works. It’s comforting, it’s safe, and even if nothingness is a perfect 72°F, it’ll feel warm and cozy to those who have been trapped in the confines of a -10°F winter.

There are a million avenues I can imagine this page going down, a million ways that you could’ve made the idea of nothing horrifying. You could expand on the horror of escapism, you could go the opposite direction and talk about how numb and dull it is in comparison to a loving and heartwarming past and you’re now stuck in endless voids of null. You could even just talk about the idea of nothingness itself and that would’ve also worked.

But this just… doesn’t.

There’s a way to make short pages good. To make them emotional, to really grab your reader by the shoulders and pull them into something so dreadful and depressing that they’re left crying behind their screen. I know because short n’ sweet is arguably my niche on the site, and what I’ve learned is that every word is valuable. Every word needs to hit the nail right on the head, because there’s so little room to work. You need to pack each phrase with so much raw emotion or else it doesn’t work. And this just didn’t for me.

I felt bad for the kid, sure, but that was just about it. You can dress up a page in fancy prose and flowery language, but literature and art needs to envoke emotion. There were so many ways you could’ve struck me down with fear and misery, use the theme of despair to your advantage, but you just didn’t.

This page is but a fragment of what it could’ve been, and for that, I’m novoting.

I hope you both receive this well, and I look forward to what you post next.

An honest review

24 Jul 2025 19:58

So, this page has a lot to talk about. I'll break it down into my initial thoughts, as well as my thoughts upon giving it a second read.

It is surprisingly short for a collab. I do understand that length does not determine quality by any means, and for the little bit that is there, it is very well-written. I love literary and descriptive writing. And I love the theme choices to convey a dreadful, yet serene nothingness. A lack of personal stylistic choices is actually why I feel that my entry from the last blitz, Teddy, is not my favorite work, not by a long shot. Was it bad? No. But I much prefer Level 175 for sticking to a theme and allowing me to write more freely (even if at the time, I was trying to stick to a very Goerman-like format). I definitely have room to grow with my works before I feel that I produce my absolute best ones. The point is, I love the touches this page chooses to include, and how it goes for very emotional imagery and descriptions.

As someone who deals with a lot of depression and existential dread, I can understand these kinds of feelings. Granted, everyone's experience with pain and trauma is different, which may explain the differing interpretations of this page and how it makes people feel. I do think that for those who went through a similar experience, this will hit close to home. I'm fortunate to have a good family, so it probably doesn't hit me as quite as relatable as it does for others. The closest experience I've ever had that pressured me into wanting to disappear in that very moment is probably my social anxiety when making speeches and the like. The deeper, darker emotions I've felt were more subtle, long-lasting ones that gave a more gloomy perspective of the world at times. There was no pressing desire to disappear in my cases, and instead, lots of times where I questioned existence and the meaning of life. Maybe wanting to sleep and not wake up on some days is somewhat comparable to wanting to see Ataraxia, even if it was just due to feeling tired of everything, as opposed to dreading it?

The psycho-analysis of this goes very deep, and I think the biggest reason it is a novote/downvote to some people is due to how it does not speak to their own personal experiences/feelings in some way. It relates to a very niche, albeit traumatic, dreadful situation. To connect it to only one kind of pain, or at least such only one such specific example of it, may be the limiting factor here, which is a symptom of it being so short. Perhaps there is a way to only give one story example and have it hit the nail on the head for more people, but I couldn't tell you how to do that. If anything is hard to predict, it is human nature. I feel like there are many different kinds of pain or intense emotions that would make someone want to retreat to Ataraxia: deep depression, great anxiety, primal dread, fight-or-flight, loss, and so much more. This stuff is no doubt all worse as a kid. Though this is also where you have irrational fears of things that can invoke dread: seeing faces in the dark, hearing sounds and thinking there is a monster under your bed, the closet nobody ever touches and the stories your mind comes up with to avoid going near it. Stuff like that. Fear is irrational, and the human experience paints a clear picture of that when you look back at things that scared you in the past. Some things are more based in reality, such as the example you gave with an abusive parental relationship, but the human mind also forms so many fears not based in reality. Anxiety and doubt are some scary things.

My experience with primal dread is a strange one. I've had that situation where I've seen faces in the dark, been afraid of great heights, feared drowning, and am gravely afraid of even the smallest of spiders. The only fears that still hold to this day are the last two, but as a kid, things like those always made me feel fear beyond any rational feeling. Sometimes I would randomly get a dreadful fear with no explanation, just sitting in the dark. Then my mind would wander "Is something watching me? Where did this sudden feeling come from? Why am I shaking?" That feels even worse than my phobias, to the point where I'm still uncertain about the nature of those moments to this day. If ever there was a moment I would want to close my eyes and disappear, that would probably have been it were it not for me being so hyper alert in those moments (though a few times, I woke up in the night, saw stuff that spooked me, and closed my eyes hoping it would go away. A heater we have used to reflect light at night in a way that looked like a creepy face. Gave me horrible shivers to think about). That is my experience in life, and why this specific situation, while definitely worthy of the feelings it evokes, does not land as close to my core as you may hope. These days, my feelings are more things like being alone in life, anxiety about the future, loss of control over my life, and the more "adult-rational fears", as opposed to the weird ones I had as a kid, outside of my phobias I still hold of course.

Anyway, I do hope that provides some insight into the crux of the matter. That was just my experience. Everyone is different. As for the writing itself, as I said, I love the style and it poses no issues to speak of on its own. The ending was nailed in terms of the uncertainty of what is going to happen next. I love narrative-driven emotional stories. I tend to watch shows and read things for that very reason. If we're looking at it from a story perspective, getting a little more "exposition" to get behind the psychology of the child *might* have helped people to relate to his feelings, at least in terms of sympathy if not empathy. As far as we know, this is just some generic kid going through a typical parental abuse situation. While it is emotional, many stories and novels do cover this type of thing, so that kind of exposure may require it to stand out on its own. I love self-insert scenes, but even then, the more personality you can inject into someone, the more relatable they become, even if they are different from you in some ways. You focused almost solely on the interaction between the parents, and only really described typical stuff like the heart pounding and the retreat of the kid, without much to speak of their own unique feelings or views about it, or for other aspects of their life that could lead to this being an even harder hitter of a situation. I would say that I'd be most curious about the thoughts racing through the kid's head, the anxiety, the fear, the things that could have led to it, maybe the outcomes he/she dreads most, etc. It still brings uncertainty, yet gives good insight into the nature of the dread they are experiencing. Stuff like a pounding heart and running away could be fight-or-flight, primal terror, or even just a desire to distance him or herself from the hurtful words and actions that made him cry. I think sadness is a possible interpretation here as much as fear. He could have already been used to seeing stuff like this and is just begging inside his mind for it to finally stop.

I'm a very introspective person, and something that hits me hard is the thought process. To know one's thoughts is to get a window into their soul. Descriptive scene setting works, but I think when you place the scene in such a way where the audience has to draw their own conclusions, that can go any number of which ways from raw description. There are some things that are universal, like a creepy atmosphere or fantastical imagery (since everyone fantasizes of the bright and vibrant, or feels uneasy around the dark and ominous), but when it comes to a situation that depends on specific experiences and feelings, if you don't set some kind of vibe to it from the character's viewpoint, that can be tricky to nail down for a larger audience. It is always important to know your audience, as well as to speak your own thoughts and feelings into the work in subtle ways, but the presentation will determine how it gets interpreted.

That's my two cents on it. Again, wonderfully written, and I am +1-ing this, since even if it didn't hit me as hard as it may some, it still is well-composed and full of raw emotion. I just think the execution could have gotten even heavier-handed and been made more impactful to people of a greater range of past experiences, especially given how people may retreat like that for more than one reason. Some enjoy doing it outside of pain, and I actually think that is the reason everyone does it. It is a means of escapism, the desire to disappear or to fantasize somewhere better. That is something general to human nature, and why I think, that no matter who reads this, it will still have some level of impact on them, even if not as great as it could be for some. That is the one act in this entire page that everyone understands on some level, no matter their history, even if they cannot get behind the reason for doing it here.

Anyway, I didn't mean to make a review that is longer than the article itself… I think it does well for the smaller audience it is likely designed for. Do I think it is the best work of yours? That's a tough one because of the metrics different people may use for rating it. In Atlas' case, Level 1.3 is more memorable to me, but that may just be due to length and more going on in it. Fear of complete nonexistence, like what the decay presents, is equally as valid as the fear of reality and wanting to be erased caused by suffering. That's such an interesting dichotomy now that I think about it. Fear of the unknown is also a potent thing.
