Antimatter Production Using Object 5 (Candy)

11 Feb 2023 04:42

So basically there's this article called [candy](object-5.md), and it lists a number of candies with different effects. One of them, the Flat Stanley, temporarily causes the user to become 2D. This has some interesting implications that the article does not even realize. You see, antimatter (that stuff that explodes with the force of E=mc2 when it touches matter) is just matter that's been flipped like a [mirror image](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CPT_symmetry). While we cannot turn 3D objects into their mirror selves, we can easily mirror 2D objects by just picking them up and flipping them over. So by turning a person 2D using the candy, we can flip them and then when they return to 3D they will be made of antimatter.

Now it's a bit more difficult to do than that since we can't just pick them up and flip them like pancakes - they're stuck to the surface. Luckily, there is a solution. The first idea we had was to project the 2D person onto a [mobius strip](https://backrooms-wiki.wikidot.com/en-wikipedia-org-wiki-non-orientable-wormhole) so we could just slide them around the strip to flip them. Unfortunately, any mobius strip we could make out of matter wouldn't be a true mobius strip since it would have thickness and would therefore distinct sides which would basically make its surface just a folded loop. It would only work if we had a mobius strip that was *truly* 2D, infinitely thin. So we started thinking about things that are infinitely thin and came up with an idea. The surface of water is infinitely thin. And since the candy allows us to project onto and 2D surface, we can project onto the surface of water. And the fun thing about water is, we can submerge a person in it. So we can project the person onto the water from below, and then project them out above, so they come out the opposite side they came in. This has the same effect as flipping them over, and should turn them into antimatter.

Now the next problem is to contain the antimatter, since if we want to use it and not just detonate it right away we need to keep it from touching any matter. The solution we came up with for this is to replace the water with mercury and the air with vacuum. We can also replace the person with a petri dish of bacteria that will consume the candy to become 2D. We then simply have an upside-down bucket submerged in mercury which we raise up partially above the level of the mercury, so the mercury in the bucket sinks down, creating a vacuum in the top of the bucket. (Mercury is heavy enough to do this.) We then insert a magnetic iron petri dish with the candy into the bucket from below and use magnets to hover it just below the surface of the mercury until it consumes the candy and becomes 2D, projecting onto the surface from below. We then raise the bucket up higher and close off the bottom so there is no longer enough room below the surface for it to become 3D again, so it must project upwards into the vacuum. I does, becoming antimatter in the process, and our magnets (which we have reversed since our magnetic petri dish has also been reversed when it became antimatter) will levitate it in place so it's not touching any matter. Then later on we can extract it from our bucket into another vacuum-sealed container and hit it with a cathode ray to annihilate its positrons and give it an electric charge so we can levitate it electrically when we need to melt the magnet to use tiny pieces of it in our generators and whatnot.

So this is a somewhat silly idea but it is also logically consistent and I think it could be used in the [Industrialization Canon](industrialization-canon-hub.md), and anyone else has any ideas like it I would like to hear them.

Wow, that is a lot of in-depth analysis of that. I like the way you think about this stuff.

Though aren't you overthinking it? I mean, it's true that "mirrored" matter is anti-matter, at least, matter with negative protons and positive electrons from what I know, which is the opposite of how atoms in matter are. Mirroring doesn't necessarily mean the spatial orientation of the matter. You can't make antimatter from flipping a 2d picture you print out of a 3d printer, so why would this be any different? If anti-matter were this easy to produce by such a means in the real world, scientists would have done so already, and anti-matter wouldn't be worth literal tens of trillions of US dollars per gram. If the mobius strip you're proposing is a spatial bend, as opposed to a physical shape alteration in whatever object it is on, then I question how one could easily and reliably bend space-time like that, even with Backrooms means. If it is just a shape of the surface it's on, then it is literally just flipping it spatially, like I said. All that would do is cause the object to face the other way when turning back to normal, would it not?

And even if you could make it, the issue with containing anti-mattter is the moment it makes contact with any matter, it will literally disintegrate both the matter it touches and itself, causing a massive explosion. This happens with a mere gram, let alone an entire object's worth of antimatter atoms. Submerging it in ANYTHING is a bad idea. You want a vacuum devoid of any liquid or air, completely devoid of atoms and molecules of any kind. The only way this is possible is an airtight room drained of all air with a magnetic field to hold said anti-matter in place (since the electrons and protons react to electromagnetism, and there would still be a positive and negative charge, even if it were flipped). It is not as simple as you make it sound, and like I said, contact with mercury will vaporize it and explode. Even then it is a risk, as truly getting rid of all air molecules and atoms floating in the air (be it from microorganisms, dust, or the air we breathe) is incredibly difficult, even with the precise tools at our real-world scientists' disposal.
