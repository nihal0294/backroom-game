30 Dec 2024 01:17

I'm just going to be up front here, and not beat around the bush with an intro.

The reason I downvoted this article is because of the prose— it doesn't flow. To give you an example of what I'm talking about, I'll write a few sentences in the style I mean.

The page feels robotic. This is because it is composed of single sentences. It feels more like a collection of facts. Thoughts are not broken with commas. Doing this would help the page significantly. I hope that this example was helpful. Sentences feel independent. There is no transition or connection between them.

I gave the page a chance however— thinking that perhaps this was some sort of commentary or deeper meaning with the level itself— perhaps something about deconstructing the psyche, or some other deep meta shit. I quickly realized this wasn't the case upon reaching the note at the end.

This brings me to my second reason for downvoting: the level just feels incomplete.

The overall content of the level description is incredibly brief. There are some cool concepts dropped here and there, as well as some good lines, but none of them are built up enough to actually land. The note we are given at the end to tie things all together feels like an afterthought— something dropped into the ending of the page in order to fill space. In a page like this, especially with things like a level, some sort of establishment of character is required in order to make someone actually *care* about the person experiencing the trauma.

Now, there are a few occasions where this isn't the case— I'll list a few, as I think they could be utilized well here.

-Exploring something from a second person perspective. (Relation becomes meaningless as everyone will have their own subjective experience— trying to develop the character of "yourself" is pointless, because by doing so all you do is alienate your readers more. It is an avenue of exploring the setting— the relation isn't the point, the imagery is. I think this could work extremely well here. Not every single thing needs character development to be considered good, as characters aren't always the focus of the story you wish to tell.)

-Writing with an already established character. (If your character has already been developed, you don't necessarily need to spend every sentence of your story continuing it. There are periods of downtime, just as there are in life. If people are already familiar with the character due to past writings, you don't need to build a relationship because it will ideally already exist.)

When you simply drop a "there is a guy," and only give us less than half a page of content, it just feels stale, similar to the "haha guys look an interview log" trope this site has.

Want my suggestion? First, try experimenting with other ways you could both present and lengthen this "note" at the end. Find a way to make people remember this character, a way to make people go "wow, that guy went through it, didn't he?" even long after they've read the page. If the main idea of your page is forgettable, why would people in-universe bother remembering it either? If you go for the second person route, cut the note entirely. Focus on crafting an experience for the reader, as if they're walking through the level and experiencing the horrors.

Keep writing  
I want to see this again

(edit: [I have tried to post this edit FIVE FUCKING TIMES and my internet keeps chain crashing I SWEAR TO GOD IF IT HAPPENS AGAIN bro] cleaning up a few things)

When you write a page about something like this, the complete obliteration of what makes someone human, you have to actually \*show\* this transition for it to land.

Zombies aren't scary because they look gross and try to eat you. Zombies are scary because you have to watch your family and loved ones slowly succumb to the disease and eventually join the horde, and knowing that one day you will join them as well.

Not only is there nothing provided to make us care about the guy, but we don't even see this descent. The page effectively starts at the climax and goes off the high dive. We never see a loss of humanity, we never see a destruction of the mind, we see the end product, and nothing to make us care.

There are plenty of levels that just straight up kill you. Those levels are usually boring, because it's just "oh ok you just die wow"

This page basically tells you the same thing. It doesn't do anything out of the ordinary for a Backrooms level, and it doesn't do anything within its own storytelling that provides a reader with anything thought-provoking. It is simply a miserable place that exists.

And a miserable place that exists can be a very good way to show these deep emotional things— but not in the Backrooms, where every other level is a miserable place equivalent to 407. Utilizing a trope or common theme is never a bad idea, but when you never go beyond the description of that trope within a setting where it is incredibly common, it just falls flat.

Here's an analogy to sort of explain what I mean by that— I'm well known as "THE GUY THAT HATES LEVELS" so this may clear things up a bit.

Imagine the Marvel cinematic universe. Imagine they want to debut a superhero. Ok cool, what does he do?

All he does is fly. That's it. Nothing else, he just flies.

Ok, well everyone and their mother can already do that and more. Why would anyone want to see a movie about "fly-guy?"

Well, you could argue because fly-guy is an interesting character. Take fucking Kite man from DC. I can't believe I'm fucking talking about Kite man as an example of good character development god damn it.

Kite man is stupid and boring. Plain and simple. He's a dude with a kite.

But he has an incredibly deep and emotional story that you can connect to, as silly as it is.

In this page, the survivor we hear from lacks that connection. It's like I said above— we don't care about him. He's just some random guy that dies or goes mad— and those are a dime a dozen as well.

I hope this edit provided some additional context
